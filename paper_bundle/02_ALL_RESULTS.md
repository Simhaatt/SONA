## Main result: VoiceBank-DEMAND 824-clip test set

`PERCEPTUAL_MP/results/phase4/table1_main_result.tex`

```
\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Post-training quantization of the GTCRN backbone on the VoiceBank-DEMAND test set (824 clips, held out from the dev split used to choose the allocation). Exempting the 3.8\,KB stem convolution from INT8 recovers essentially all of the quantization loss and yields a model that is statistically indistinguishable from FP32 ($p=0.25$). It is also \emph{smaller} than uniform INT8: quantizing every layer makes ONNX Runtime insert QDQ pairs around $143$ additional activation tensors ($172$ vs.\ $29$ QDQ nodes), whose graph overhead exceeds the stem's $2.8$\,KB of weight savings. The mixed allocation therefore dominates uniform INT8 on both size and quality.}
```

## E2 — findings

`PERCEPTUAL_MP/results/hessian/FINDINGS_HESSIAN.md`

### E2 — Hessian-trace sensitivity (HAWQ-V2): findings

Script: `PERCEPTUAL_MP/scripts/hessian_ranking.py`. Hutchinson estimator, 32 Rademacher probes per
clip over 50 dev clips (p254/p256, speaker-disjoint from TRAIN — no test data touched), 1 600
estimates per layer.

---

#### 1. Why this experiment exists

Study 1's negative result is that a *perceptual* (PESQ) sensitivity ranking agrees with
*output-error* rankings (SI-SDR, mask-MSE). But the field-standard way to rank layers for
mixed-precision allocation is neither: HAWQ and HAWQ-V2 rank by second-order curvature. Without
that column, the negative result never compares against the baseline a reviewer actually has in
mind.

#### 2. A correction made during this experiment

The first pass ranked layers by **average Hessian trace**, `tr(H)/n`, alone. That is *not* the
HAWQ-V2 criterion. HAWQ-V2 ranks by the expected loss perturbation from quantizing the layer:

```
Omega_l = (tr(H_l) / n_l) * || Q(w_l) - w_l ||^2
```

The second factor — the actual INT8 perturbation of that layer's weights — is what carries
parameter count and weight scale. Dropping it compares curvature *per weight* while ignoring how
many weights there are, which is precisely the axis on which this model is pathological: the head
has **97** parameters and each block has **166 080**.

Ranking on the incomplete criterion produced a striking but wrong conclusion (that HAWQ-V2
misranks). The corrected criterion reverses it. Reporting the incomplete version would have been a
strawman, and a reviewer would rightly have said the baseline was implemented incorrectly.

#### 3. Result

| rank | layer | params | tr(H) | avg tr(H)/n | ‖Q(w)−w‖² | **Ω** |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | **stem** | 1 152 | 4.79e+02 | 4.16e-01 | 2.66e-03 | **1.11e-03** |
| 2 | block3_d8 | 166 080 | 4.48e+02 | 2.70e-03 | 4.81e-02 | 1.30e-04 |
| 3 | block2_d4 | 166 080 | 4.58e+02 | 2.76e-03 | 4.53e-02 | 1.25e-04 |
| 4 | block0_d1 | 166 080 | 1.81e+02 | 1.09e-03 | 7.59e-02 | 8.25e-05 |
| 5 | block1_d2 | 166 080 | 1.25e+02 | 7.50e-04 | 6.56e-02 | 4.92e-05 |
| 6 | **head** | 97 | 8.62e+01 | 8.88e-01 | 3.18e-05 | **2.82e-05** |
| — | band_gru *(unquantizable)* | 55 872 | 5.49e+02 | 9.82e-03 | 6.68e-02 | 6.56e-04 |

**The full HAWQ-V2 criterion puts `stem` first and `head` last — exactly matching the empirical
INT8 damage ranking.**

##### Rank agreement with the measured per-layer damage

| criterion | vs ΔSI-SDR | vs mask-MSE | vs ΔSTOI | vs ΔESTOI | vs ΔLSD |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Ω (full HAWQ-V2)** | **+0.600** | +0.486 | +0.600 | +0.429 | +0.486 |
| raw tr(H) | **+0.714** | +0.543 | +0.657 | +0.486 | +0.429 |
| avg tr(H)/n *(incomplete)* | −0.143 | −0.314 | −0.200 | −0.371 | −0.429 |

#### 4. What this means for the paper

**The negative result becomes comprehensive rather than partial.** Three independent families of
sensitivity criterion — perceptual (PESQ), output-error (SI-SDR, mask-MSE), and second-order
curvature (HAWQ-V2) — all identify the same layer as most sensitive and the same layer as least.
The criterion does not matter. That is a stronger version of Study 1's claim than "two output-error
metrics agree with a perceptual one", and it closes the obvious reviewer objection.

**A practical caveat worth one sentence in the paper.** *Average* Hessian trace is often used as
shorthand for HAWQ-V2 sensitivity. Here that shorthand **inverts the ranking**: it promotes the
97-parameter head from least to most sensitive, purely because dividing by 97 inflates it against
blocks divided by 166 080. When layer parameter counts span three orders of magnitude, the
perturbation term is not optional. Implement the full criterion.

#### 5. Limitations — state these

- **n = 6 layers.** No correlation here reaches significance (p = 0.11–0.40). The claim rests on
  the agreement of the rank *orderings* — particularly that all three criteria agree on the top and
  bottom layer — and on the mechanism, not on a p-value. Say so explicitly rather than quoting ρ as
  though six points settled it.
- Hutchinson is a stochastic estimator; per-layer standard errors are recorded in
  `hessian_ranking.csv`.
- The band GRU is reported for completeness but excluded from the ranking comparison, exactly as it
  is excluded from the allocation search — it cannot be quantized on this runtime.
- Curvature is measured at the trained optimum under the training loss (SI-SDR + 1.25·log-mag +
  0.25·multi-resolution). A different loss would give different curvature.

**This experiment was run knowing it could weaken the paper's headline.** It did not.

---

#### 1. Result — the finding replicates

| | magnitude GTCRN | complex GTCRN |
|---|---|---|
| most sensitive layer | **stem** | **stem** |
| least sensitive layer | **head** | **head** |
| stem's share of INT8 degradation | **98.7 %** | **92.1 %** |
| uniform INT8 ΔSI-SDR | −0.109 dB | −0.051 dB |
| mixed (stem FP32) ΔSI-SDR | −0.0014 dB (n.s.) | +0.0041 dB |
| uniform INT8 size | 950.7 KB | 952.2 KB |
| **mixed size** | **916.9 KB** | **917.4 KB** |

Per-layer sensitivity, mean absolute ΔSI-SDR against FP32:

| layer | mean abs ΔSI-SDR |
|---|---:|
| **stem** | **0.0176** |
| block0_d1 | 0.0124 |
| block1_d2 | 0.0086 |
| block3_d8 | 0.0086 |
| block2_d4 | 0.0083 |
| head | 0.0067 |

Three things transfer:

1. **The stem dominates.** Most sensitive of the six on both models, and the head is least
   sensitive on both.
2. **The exemption works.** Removing the stem from the INT8 set takes the loss from −0.051 dB to
   +0.004 dB.
3. **The size inversion replicates.** The mixed model is *smaller* than uniform INT8 (917.4 vs
   952.2 KB) on this architecture too, for the same reason — quantizing every layer makes the
   runtime insert QDQ pairs around many more activation tensors, and that graph overhead exceeds
   the stem's weight savings.

#### 2. What this does and does not license

**Does:** the claim moves from one checkpoint to two models, so "the stem carries the damage" is no
longer a single-checkpoint observation. The reviewer objection that motivated this experiment is
answered.

**Does not:** both models share the *same backbone* — stem, four gated TF-conv blocks, band GRU —
and differ only in the output head (2-channel complex ratio mask, +97 parameters). This is a
replication across **output representations**, not across architecture families. It does not show
the finding holds for a differently-shaped denoiser.

State it that way in the paper. The honest scope is: *robust to the output representation, untested
across backbone families.* A genuinely independent test would need something like DeepFilterNet2,
whose enhanced outputs are already in the repo but which was never put through the per-layer sweep.

#### 3. One thing our own diagnostic flags

The mixed model scores **+0.0041 dB above FP32** on this dev split, with a bootstrap CI of
[+0.0000, +0.0080] that only marginally excludes zero.

By the dither rule established in R3 — *a lossy approximation cannot be better than the model it
approximates, so a variant beating FP32 means the metric is compromised on that data* — this cell
should be flagged rather than reported as an improvement. The effect is tiny and the CI touches
zero, so the correct reading is "indistinguishable from FP32", the same conclusion as the magnitude
model, **not** "better than FP32".

Applying our own rule against our own favourable result is the consistent thing to do, and it is
worth a sentence in the paper for exactly that reason.

#### 4. Caveats

- Dev split only (200 clips). The magnitude model's headline numbers come from the 824-clip test
  set; this replication was not run on test, so the two columns in §1 are not measured on the same
  data. Comparable in direction, not directly comparable in magnitude.
- The complex checkpoint was trained on a 5 000-clip subset, not the full corpus, so its absolute
  quality is below the locked model. The *relative* per-layer pattern is what transfers.
- ONNX export for this model required pinning the legacy TorchScript exporter: torch 2.13's dynamo
  path emits `Split` with a `num_outputs` attribute that ONNX Runtime 1.20.1 cannot load during
  static quantization. Recorded in `scripts/export_onnx.py` behind `--dynamo`.

### Dither diagnostic

Post-training quantization approximates a source model, so **FP32 is a ceiling, not a competitor**. Any metric on which a quantized variant significantly beats FP32 is not measuring approximation quality on that corpus -- the extra quantization noise is acting as a dither. ## E3 — findings

`results/breakdowns/wer_significance/FINDINGS_WER.md`

### WER significance — findings (E3, 2026-08-20)

Two standard ways of aggregating WER over the same 824 clips, and they **disagree about which
conditions are affected and whether the aggregate effect exists at all**. That disagreement is not
a nuisance to resolve quietly; it is the paper's thesis reproducing itself inside a single leg.

Scripts: `scripts/wer_condition_significance.py` (per-clip), `scripts/wer_corpus_level.py`
(corpus-level). Baseline is `passthrough` throughout; positive difference = enhancement made WER
**worse**.

---

#### 1. What each analysis says about GTCRN

| comparison | per-clip mean of rates | corpus-level (Σerr/Σwords) |
|---|---|---|
| **overall** | +11.1 %, p = 0.270 — **null** | **+24.1 %, CI excludes 0 — significant** |
| **bus** | **−41.7 %, p = 0.021** (q = 0.095) | −28.0 %, CI includes 0 — **not significant** |
| **cafe** | +43.8 %, p = 0.038 (q = 0.095) | **+57.9 %, CI excludes 0 — significant** |
| living / office / psquare | not significant | not significant |
| **SNR 2.5 dB** | +33.7 %, p = 0.095 | **+43.1 %, CI excludes 0 — significant** |

#### 2. The corrected claim

The previously recorded finding — *"cuts WER 41 % in bus against a 44 % rise in cafe"* — was based
on point estimates only and **does not survive testing as stated**. Two changes are required:

1. **Drop the bus improvement.** It is significant per-clip at nominal p, but does not survive
   BH-FDR within the 5-noise-type family (q = 0.095), and the better-powered corpus-level analysis
   does not find it at all. There is no defensible claim that enhancement improves ASR in `bus`.
2. **Keep, and strengthen, the degradation.** Enhancement significantly *hurts* ASR overall
   (+24.1 % relative), and the damage is concentrated in **cafe babble** (+57.9 %) and at the
   **lowest SNR** (2.5 dB, +43.1 %).

Corrected statement for the paper:

> Enhancement significantly degrades ASR accuracy overall (+24 % relative corpus WER), and the
> degradation is concentrated: babble noise and the lowest-SNR bin account for essentially all of
> it, while the remaining three noise types show no significant effect. The apparent improvement in
> `bus` noise visible in per-clip averages is not statistically supported.

This is *still* a concentration result, and the classical-baseline finding is untouched and much
stronger — see §4.

#### 3. Why the two analyses disagree, and why it matters

Per-clip averaging treats a 3-word utterance and a 36-word utterance as equal evidence. Corpus-level
weights by reference length, which is what the aggregate is supposed to mean.

The corpus has **6 183 reference words over 824 clips, median 7 words per clip**. Utterances are
short and Whisper transcribes most of them perfectly under both methods, so only **129 / 824**
pairs differ at all. Inside one noise condition the effective sample falls to **16–37 clips**,
which is why the per-clip test is underpowered against effects that look large.

The sign flip on `bus` follows directly: the per-clip improvement sits on short clips that
corpus-level aggregation correctly down-weights.

**The methodological point is reportable in its own right:** on short-utterance corpora, per-clip
WER averaging and corpus-level WER can disagree about *both* the existence of an aggregate effect
and the sign of a per-condition effect. Papers reporting "WER" almost never say which they used.

#### 4. The classical-baseline finding is unaffected and very strong

Robust under both analyses and after FDR correction — 19 / 40 comparisons survive BH, and every
one of them is a classical method degrading WER:

| method | condition | per-clip diff | q |
|---|---|---|---|
| wiener | office | +243.8 % | 0.0000 |
| wiener | overall | +127.6 % | 0.0000 |
| wiener | cafe | +121.3 % | 0.0000 |
| wiener | snr 7.5 | +200.0 % | 0.0000 |
| spectral subtraction | snr 7.5 | +95.5 % | 0.0022 |

Classical baselines are worse than doing nothing on ASR in **every** condition tested, at effect
sizes an order of magnitude beyond the neural model's. This remains the most robust claim in the
leg.

#### 5. Remedy — how to recover the power that was lost

The effect sizes are large; the problem is purely that too few clips differ. Three fixes, in order
of cost:

1. **Report corpus-level WER as primary** (done here, zero cost). Recovers the aggregate result
   the per-clip test could not detect.
2. **Add MS-SNSD to the WER evaluation** — 1 100 further clips with noise-type labels, roughly
   doubling n and adding noise categories VoiceBank does not cover. Needs one Whisper pass.
3. **Use a smaller ASR model as a second operating point.** Whisper is near-ceiling on this
   clean-ish read speech, which is the direct cause of the zero-inflation. A weaker model raises
   the error floor and multiplies the effective sample. Report both, since "does enhancement help
   ASR" is genuinely ASR-model-dependent — and that dependence is itself on-thesis.

Fix 1 is applied. Fixes 2 and 3 need a Whisper pass and are queued for the Kaggle batch alongside
the timing benchmark.

## Complex-mask A/B

`sona-final-month/COMPLEX_MASK_FINDINGS.md`

### Complex Ratio Mask vs Magnitude Mask — Controlled A/B

Tests the hypothesis: predicting a **complex** ratio mask (modifying magnitude AND
phase) beats the shipped **magnitude-only** gain mask (which preserves the noisy
phase), without a dramatically larger backbone.

#### What was built

A new denoiser head + apply path, added to the main repo behind a clean dispatch
so the existing magnitude path is untouched:

- `src/models/gtcrn.py :: ComplexGTCRNMask` — **identical** GTCRN backbone (stem +
  4 gated TF-conv blocks + band GRU) as the locked `GTCRNMask`; the only change is
  a 2-channel head predicting the real & imaginary parts of a bounded complex ratio
  mask (`bound * tanh`, bound=2). **+97 params** (722,306 vs 722,209).
- `src/models/torch_frontend.py :: apply_band_complex` — complex-multiplies the
  per-band mask with the noisy spectrum (`enh = M * Y`), so both magnitude and phase
  change. The magnitude path (`apply_band_gain`) multiplies a real gain and keeps
  the noisy phase.
- Dispatch on `BaseGainModel.is_complex` in the training step and `NeuralEnhancer`.
- Registered as model kind `complex_gtcrn`.
- 4 unit tests (identity-mask reconstruction, shapes/bound, overfit, enhancer
  roundtrip) — all green; full 56-test suite passes.

#### Two engineering fixes that mattered (documented honestly)

1. **Identity initialisation.** A tanh complex head starts near mask=0 (silence),
   vs the sigmoid magnitude head which starts near a 0.5 gain (half pass-through).
   The complex head is initialised (bias → real=1, imag=0; head weight scaled 0.1)
   to start as a near pass-through — a fair starting point with healthy gradients.
   Scaling 0.01 started cleaner but throttled gradients and crippled learning
   (measured), so 0.1 was chosen.
2. **Scale-sensitive SNR loss term.** The training loss is SI-SDR, which is
   scale-INVARIANT — so the complex mask (which controls magnitude directly) drifted
   the output level, tanking the scale-sensitive SNR (−6.6 dB) while SI-SDR was fine.
   Adding a small `snr_weight=0.3` term (`src/train/losses.py :: snr_loss`), applied
   identically to both models, anchored the level and flipped SNR to +0.8 (8-epoch).
   A good enhancer should preserve absolute level, so this is a legitimate fix, not
   a thumb on the scale — and it is applied to the magnitude baseline too.

#### Method

Both models trained under IDENTICAL conditions — same 1200-clip VoiceBank-DEMAND
train subset, same 40 epochs, same loss (SI-SDR + 1.25·log-mag + 0.25·multi-res-mag
+ 0.3·SNR), same optimiser/seed, same ch=96/hidden=96/4-block backbone. The ONLY
variable is the output representation. This is the fair test — comparing against the
locked Kaggle checkpoint (50 epochs, full data) would be confounded by training
budget, not the mask type.

Evaluated on 400 VoiceBank test clips. Local metrics: SI-SDR(i), SNR(i), STOI,
ESTOI, LSD. (PESQ needs a C build unavailable for this Python; DNSMOS needs a
Kaggle-only module — both are computed on Kaggle in the main pipeline.)

## E1 / E1b — findings

`AudioVQ/results/ratio_at_k/FINDINGS_RATIO.md`

### E1 / E1b — does a label-free geometric metric predict task retention?

Scripts: `AudioVQ/scripts/ratio_at_k.py`, `AudioVQ/scripts/metric_predicts_task.py`.
3 embedding models × 19 methods; 36 points where a geometric metric and measured downstream
retention are both available.

---

#### 1. The question

The paper argues Recall@k understates deployment quality under quantization. A practitioner's
immediate reply: *then what should I measure, short of running the downstream task — which needs
labels my deployment may not have?*

Two candidates, both purely geometric and label-free:

- **Recall@10** — the field default.
- **1/Ratio@10** — the inverse approximation ratio proposed by Dimitropoulos & Mamoulis
  (arXiv:2606.04522) for ANN *search*, computed here under *quantization*.

Ground truth is measured semantic retention: downstream speaker- and noise-type-retrieval precision
after quantization, divided by its float32 value.

#### 2. Result

| metric | mean abs error vs semantic retention | Pearson r | Spearman ρ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Recall@10 | 0.4280 | +0.754 | +0.689 |
| **1/Ratio@10** | **0.0328** | +0.732 | +0.674 |

**1/Ratio@10 is ~13× better calibrated than Recall@10** (0.033 vs 0.428 mean absolute error), while
the two rank methods almost identically.

The precise statement, which matters: **Recall@k preserves the *ordering* of quantizers but not the
*calibration*; 1/Ratio@k preserves both.** Recall will tell you which quantizer is better. It will
badly misinform you about how much you are losing.

At the paper's headline operating point (1 bit/coordinate, ~30× compression) on WavLM:

| | value |
|---|---|
| Recall@10 | 0.483 |
| 1/Ratio@10 | 0.970 |
| measured semantic retention | 0.965 |

The label-free geometric metric lands within half a point of the task measurement that recall
misses by 48 points.

#### 3. The discrimination check — and why it was needed

An earlier version of this analysis covered only 12 points, all with semantic retention ≥ 0.96. A
high correlation over a narrow range is weak evidence however large the coefficient, and a metric
pinned near 1.0 everywhere would look calibrated on good quantizers while being useless in
practice.

Two fixes widened the range: an alias bug meant the `no-rot` variants never matched (the semantic
CSV uses a space, not a hyphen), and the uniform scalar quantizer was absent from the semantic
sweep entirely although the code already supported it. Uniform 1-bit destroys retrieval, so
including it is the case that decides whether the metric discriminates.

| model | Recall@10 | 1/Ratio@10 | semantic retention |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| WavLM | 0.0443 | 0.828 | 0.889 |
| wav2vec2 | 0.0133 | 0.720 | 0.953 |
| HuBERT | 0.0058 | 0.777 | 0.946 |

**1/Ratio@10 does discriminate** — it drops to 0.72–0.83 on the broken quantizer rather than
sitting at 1.0. It is *conservative* there, under-predicting retention by 0.13–0.23, which is the
safe direction for a deployment metric.

#### 4. An unplanned result that is stronger than the headline

Uniform 1-bit loses **95–99 % of true nearest neighbours** (Recall@10 = 0.006–0.044) while retaining
**89–95 % of downstream task accuracy**.

The geometry–meaning dissociation the paper is built on is therefore not a mild effect at moderate
compression. At aggressive compression it is close to total: the neighbour list is essentially
destroyed and the task barely notices. This is the most extreme demonstration in the project and it
came out of a check added to guard against a weak correlation, not from the planned sweep.

#### 5. What to claim

1. Recall@k is well-ordered but badly calibrated under quantization; report 1/Ratio@k alongside it.
2. 1/Ratio@k is label-free, so it is available to deployments that cannot run a labelled downstream
   evaluation — which is the practical contribution.
3. The dissociation strengthens with compression, up to ~99 % neighbour loss at ~9 % task loss.

#### 6. Limitations

- 36 points across 3 models and 4 bit-widths; not a general claim about all quantizers.
- Semantic retention here means speaker and noise-type retrieval on two corpora. Other downstream
  tasks may weight the neighbour list differently — a task that genuinely needs exact nearest
  neighbours would not show this.
- 1/Ratio@k under-predicts on badly broken quantizers, so it is a safety-biased estimate rather
  than an unbiased one.
- Both metrics are computed on 10 000-vector indices; §5.6 of the paper already shows behaviour
  shifts with index size.

## R1 — findings

`results/condition_gating/FINDINGS_R1.md`

### R1 — condition-gated enhancement: findings

Remedy for the ASR negative result. Script: `scripts/condition_gated_enhancement.py`.
Corpus-level WER (Σ errors / Σ reference words) on the 824-clip VoiceBank test set.

#### Result

| routing | corpus WER | vs always-enhance |
|---|---|---|
| always enhance (current behaviour) | 0.0428 | — |
| **never enhance** | **0.0345** | **−0.0083** |
| oracle, per noise type (upper bound, not deployable) | 0.0333 | −0.0095 |
| learned router, leave-one-noise-type-out | 0.0370 | −0.0058 |

The router captures **61 % of the oracle headroom**, and its improvement over always-enhance is
statistically supported: paired clip bootstrap 95 % CI **[−0.0119, −0.0003]**, excluding zero.

Per-fold behaviour is coherent — the router was never shown the held-out noise type, and still
routed `bus` mostly to the enhancer (only 14 % to passthrough, matching the oracle's `enhance`) and
`living` mostly away from it (86 % to passthrough, matching `passthrough`).

#### The honest recommendation is simpler than the router

The oracle enhances **only** `bus`, and `bus`'s advantage is small: the gap between never-enhance
(0.0345) and the oracle (0.0333) is just 0.0012 WER. Almost all of the achievable gain comes from
*not enhancing*, not from routing cleverly.

So the deployment recommendation is blunt:

> **Do not put the enhancer in the ASR path.** Feed the recogniser the unprocessed signal and the
> human listener the enhanced one. Enhancement is worth +9.9 dB SI-SDRi and +0.009 STOI to a
> person; it costs +24 % relative WER to a recogniser. These are different consumers with
> different optima, and the system should stop pretending one signal serves both.

The router is worth reporting because it shows the decision is *learnable from the noisy audio
alone* and generalises to an unseen noise type — but on this corpus it does not beat simply
switching the enhancer off for ASR. We say so rather than presenting the router as the winner.

#### Why this is on-thesis

The aggregate metric said "enhancement costs 24 % WER". Disaggregating showed the cost is
concentrated in four of five noise types, with one type actually benefiting. Acting on the
disaggregated view — by routing, or more simply by removing the enhancer from the ASR path —
recovers the loss. The remedy is only visible once the aggregate is broken apart, which is the
paper's argument in miniature.

#### Limitations

- Five noise types is a small number of folds; the leave-one-out estimate is coarse.
- The router optimises WER only. A deployment that cares about both listeners would need a
  cost-weighted objective, which we have not fitted.
- Labels are derived from a single ASR model (Whisper `small.en`). The queued `tiny.en` run tests
  whether the routing decision is stable across recognisers; if the noise types that hurt differ by
  ASR model, the recommendation to keep the enhancer out of the ASR path gets *stronger*, not
  weaker.

## Kaggle Phase A — findings (R2 timing, W2/W3 power fixes)

`results/kaggle_phase_a/FINDINGS_KAGGLE.md`

### Kaggle Phase A batch — findings

Run 2026-08-20 on Kaggle. **Intel Xeon @ 2.00 GHz, 4 cores, 1-min load average 0.41** (idle) —
against the local AMD Ryzen 5 5600H, so the timing results double as a cross-architecture check.
ONNX Runtime pinned to 1.20.1 as everywhere else.

---

#### R2 — the stem exemption is smaller, faster, *and* FP32-quality

| backend | threads | mean RTF | slowdown vs FP32 | mean SI-SDR | size |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FP32 | 1 | 0.0894 | — | 18.690 | 2842.7 KB |
| uniform INT8 | 1 | 0.1244 | 1.392× | 18.569 | 950.7 KB |
| **mixed (stem FP32)** | 1 | **0.1181** | **1.322×** | **18.691** | **916.9 KB** |
| FP32 | 2 | 0.0819 | — | 18.690 | 2842.7 KB |
| uniform INT8 | 2 | 0.0986 | 1.204× | 18.569 | 950.7 KB |
| **mixed** | 2 | **0.0944** | **1.153×** | **18.691** | **916.9 KB** |
| FP32 | 4 | 0.0837 | — | 18.690 | 2842.7 KB |
| uniform INT8 | 4 | 0.0994 | 1.188× | 18.569 | 950.7 KB |
| **mixed** | 4 | **0.0948** | **1.133×** | **18.691** | **916.9 KB** |

**The prediction held.** Mixed carries 29 QDQ nodes against uniform INT8's 172, and it is faster at
every thread count — by 4–5 % consistently. So exempting the stem gives a model that is
simultaneously:

- **smaller** than uniform INT8 (916.9 vs 950.7 KB),
- **faster** than uniform INT8 (1.13–1.32× vs 1.19–1.39× slowdown),
- **quality-identical to FP32** (18.691 vs 18.690 SI-SDR, against uniform's 18.569).

This converts the study's "INT8 is slower than FP32" negative into a positive: the graph overhead
that causes the slowdown is itself concentrated, and the same exemption that recovers the quality
also recovers part of the speed. It is the concentration thesis showing up in a third quantity
after loss and file size.

**Cross-architecture replication.** INT8 is slower than FP32 on both CPUs tested — 1.19–1.39× on
Intel Xeon, 1.39–1.46× on AMD Ryzen. The finding is not an artifact of one machine. Absolute RTFs
differ, as expected, and both are now measured on verified-idle machines.

---

#### W2 / W3 — the power fixes worked, and they change a claim

Effective sample — clips whose WER actually differs between GTCRN and passthrough:

| evaluation | effective n | vs original |
|---|---|---|
| VoiceBank, `small.en` (original) | 129 / 824 = 15.7 % | — |
| VoiceBank, `tiny.en` | 216 / 824 = 26.2 % | 1.7× |
| **MS-SNSD, `small.en`** | **704 / 1100 = 64.0 %** | **5.5×** |

##### The aggregate degradation is now firmly established

Enhancement significantly raises corpus WER on **three independent measurements**:

| evaluation | Δ corpus WER | relative | CI excludes 0 |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| VoiceBank / `small.en` | +0.0083 | +24.1 % | yes |
| VoiceBank / `tiny.en` | +0.0165 | +23.1 % | yes |
| MS-SNSD / `small.en` | +0.0641 | +46.6 % | yes |

The two VoiceBank figures agree closely (+24.1 % and +23.1 %) despite different recognisers, which
is strong evidence the effect is real and not an artifact of one ASR model.

##### A claim that must be softened: "concentration" was partly a power artifact

The earlier reading — *degradation concentrated in babble and the lowest SNR bin* — does not
survive better power. On MS-SNSD, **8 of 9 noise types are individually significant**:

| noise | relative Δ | significant |
|---|---:|---|
| CopyMachine | +76.1 % | yes |
| Babble | +44.2 % | yes |
| AirportAnnouncements | +78.4 % | yes |
| ShuttingDoor | +94.3 % | yes |
| Munching | +80.4 % | yes |
| AirConditioner | +67.6 % | yes |
| VacuumCleaner | +41.8 % | yes |
| Neighbor | +12.4 % | yes |
| **Typing** | +23.2 % | **no** |

With 5.5× the effective sample, enhancement hurts ASR **nearly everywhere**, not in a couple of
conditions. The VoiceBank per-condition nulls were largely a lack of power, not evidence that those
conditions are unaffected.

Corrected statement:

> Enhancement significantly degrades ASR accuracy, replicated across two corpora and two
> recognisers (+23 % to +47 % relative). The degradation is broad rather than concentrated: with
> adequate statistical power, 8 of 9 MS-SNSD noise types show it individually. The only exception
> is `Typing` — impulsive noise, where the enhancer barely acts at all (+0.2 dB SI-SDRi in the
> objective benchmark), so there is little processing to do damage.

Note this is the opposite direction to the rest of the paper: here the *aggregate* was right and the
disaggregation initially misled us, because the per-condition tests were underpowered. That is worth
stating plainly — a paper about metrics misleading should be first to report when its own
disaggregation was the misleading step.

##### The `bus` improvement is definitively an artifact

Across three measurements of the same clips it does not hold a sign:

| measurement | `bus` relative Δ | significant |
|---|---:|---|
| VoiceBank `small.en`, per-clip | −41.7 % (helps) | fails FDR (q = 0.095) |
| VoiceBank `small.en`, corpus | −28.0 % (helps) | no |
| VoiceBank `tiny.en`, corpus | **+37.2 % (hurts)** | no |

A sign flip between recognisers on identical audio settles it. The earlier correction was right and
now has independent support.

#### R1 remedy re-tested — and it gets simpler

Re-running condition-gated enhancement against the `tiny.en` labels:

| routing | corpus WER |
|---|---:|
| always enhance | 0.0876 |
| learned router (leave-one-noise-type-out) | 0.0730 |
| **never enhance** | **0.0711** |
| oracle, per noise type | 0.0711 |

**The oracle chooses passthrough for all five noise types.** Under this recogniser there is no
condition where enhancement helps ASR, so the oracle and never-enhance coincide exactly.

The router still beats always-enhance significantly (CI [−0.0215, −0.0083], 89 % of headroom
captured) but cannot beat simply switching the enhancer off. Combined with the MS-SNSD result, the
recommendation is now unambiguous and needs no routing machinery at all:

> **Keep the enhancer out of the ASR path.** Give the recogniser the unprocessed signal and the
> human listener the enhanced one. They are different consumers with different optima.

More data made the recommendation *simpler*, not more elaborate. The routing experiment was still
worth running — it establishes that the decision is learnable from noisy audio alone and
generalises to an unseen noise type — but it is reported as a negative for routing, not a win.

## Scene classifier

`sona-final-month/SCENE_CLASSIFIER_FINDINGS.md`

### Scene Classifier — Real-Audio Evaluation & Learned Upgrade

Layer B item (plan Week 2 / SONA_STATUS open item 7): score the scene classifier
on real audio and fix it if the labels are embarrassing. They were. This is the
full evidence trail, including the one place a learned model was justified.

#### 1. Real-audio evaluation (was: synthetic self-test only)

Built a labelled real-audio set from MS-SNSD, whose filenames encode the true
noise type (`scripts/build_scene_manifest.py`). Only categories with an
unambiguous SONA scene label were scored:

| scene   | MS-SNSD sources                                   | clips |
|---------|---------------------------------------------------|------:|
| fan     | AirConditioner, VacuumCleaner, WasherDryer        | 38    |
| crowd   | Babble, Cafeteria, Restaurant, …                  | 25    |
| speech  | clean_test/clnsp*.wav                             | 30    |
| traffic | Traffic, Car, Bus, Metro, Station                 | 5     |

`music` has no MS-SNSD source — reported as a coverage gap, not counted as a miss.

**Shipped heuristic on real pure-source audio: 0.469 accuracy** (vs 0.767 on the
old synthetic self-test). Two hand-tuned windows were measurably *inverted*:
- `crowd` rewarded `high_ratio ∈ [0.12, 0.55]`, but real babble measures ~0.03.
- `speech` carried a `−0.5·high` penalty, though real speech has the *highest*
  HF energy (~0.14) of any class.

#### 2. Threshold retune (heuristic, no learning)

The classifier is a deterministic hand-tuned scorer with no learned weights, so
fine-tuning / distillation do not apply — the lever is the `_tri()` windows.
Re-seated them on measured per-class quantiles and refined by balanced-accuracy
coordinate search under stratified 5-fold CV (`scripts/tune_scene_thresholds.py`).

| set                                   | accuracy (CV)     |
|---------------------------------------|-------------------|
| shipped heuristic                     | 0.469             |
| data-seeded windows (no search)       | 0.694             |
| tuned heuristic (pure sources)        | **0.714 ± 0.025** |

Most of the gain is from correct seeding, not fitting — so it is not overfit.
Applied to `src/controller/scene.py`: crowd recall 0.04→0.76, fan 0.29→0.66.

#### 3. The train/serve mismatch (why pure-source tuning was not enough)

Production input is *noisy speech*, not pure noise or clean speech. Evaluated on
320 speech+noise mixtures across 0–15 dB SNR (`scripts/build_scene_mixtures.py`):

- tuned heuristic on mixtures (all SNR):        **0.409**
- tuned heuristic on noise-dominant (≤5 dB):    **0.490 ± 0.072 CV**

At high SNR the speech masks the noise character, so the noise-environment label
becomes genuinely ambiguous — an inherent ceiling for 8 spectral features under a
hand-written rule. The heuristic barely beats the 0.422 majority-class baseline.

#### 4. Where a learned model IS justified

Holding the *same 8 features* fixed and swapping only the decision rule, under an
identical 5-fold protocol (`scripts/compare_scene_learned.py`), on the
noise-dominant mixtures:

| decision rule        | accuracy (CV)     | balanced |
|----------------------|-------------------|----------|
| majority baseline    | 0.422             | 0.250    |
| **tuned heuristic**  | **0.490**         | 0.539    |
| logistic regression  | 0.717             | 0.656    |
| random forest (200)  | 0.800             | 0.728    |
| **MLP (32,16)**      | **0.844 ± 0.052** | 0.804    |

**The decision rule, not the feature set, was the bottleneck (+0.35).** This is
the one place in the SONA pipeline where replacing a hand-tuned rule with a
learned one is evidence-backed rather than assumed.

#### 5. Honest re-evaluation: grouped CV + richer features (supersedes §4)

The 0.844 in §4 used **per-clip** StratifiedKFold. With only ~5 traffic and a
handful of fan/crowd noise textures, mixtures of the *same* texture can land in
both train and test — the model can memorise a texture rather than learn the
scene. The honest protocol groups every split by **noise source file**
(`StratifiedGroupKFold`), so no texture is ever on both sides.

Under that protocol, and comparing the shipped 8 features against a **richer
34-feature set** (temporal spread + deltas + finer spectral bands, see
`scripts/scene_feature_lib.py`), searched over 10 model families
(`scripts/scene_search.py`) on 611 clips / 108 source groups:

| features | best model | honest grouped-CV acc | optimistic per-clip |
|----------|-----------|----------------------:|--------------------:|
| base 8   | random forest | 0.748             | 0.796               |
| **rich 34** | **MLP(32,16)** | **0.773**       | 0.895               |

Averaged over **5 seeds × 5 folds** for stability (`finalize_scene_model.py`),
the finalists settle to:

| model            | honest acc        | balanced |
|------------------|-------------------|----------|
| MLP(32,16)       | **0.748 ± 0.074** | 0.705    |
| logreg (balanced)| 0.708             | 0.753    |
| svm-rbf          | 0.742             | 0.727    |
| logreg+MLP vote  | 0.754             | 0.729    |

**The honest, texture-leakage-free, multi-seed accuracy is ~0.75** — not the
0.813 reported in §4, which was optimistic (per-clip, single seed, smaller set).
The correction is the point: grouped CV is what a reviewer would demand.

#### 6. Shipped model

The MLP(32,16) on the 34 rich features (within 1 std of the best ensemble, and
the only finalist with a clean plain-numpy export) is shipped as **1,716
parameters** (`finalize_scene_model.py` → `src/controller/scene_model.py`):

- **Honest grouped-CV accuracy: 0.748 ± 0.074** (5 seeds × 5 folds, 611 clips,
  108 source groups). The product `SceneClassifier.features` computes the exact
  34 features the model was trained on (verified bit-identical to the training
  extractor, max diff 0.0).
- Inference needs no scikit-learn and no torch — stays local and edge-deployable.
- `SceneClassifier` uses it by default and **falls back to the tuned heuristic**
  if `scene_model.py` is absent (`use_learned=False`). Both paths are unit-tested.
- GTCRN denoising itself (Layer A, locked) was re-verified objectively:
  +5.8–6.3 dB SI-SDRi on AirConditioner, +3.4–4.1 dB on Babble, +0.2 dB on
  Typing (impulsive noise is an inherent spectral-masking limitation).

### Phase D — MOS listening test runbook

Everything is built and validated. What is missing is **human raters**. This is the only part of
the plan that cannot be done by running something.

---

#### What you need

| | |
|---|---|
| **Raters** | **18–20 people.** See the power note below for why not 13. |
| Time per rater | **12–18 minutes** (50 clips, one rating each) |
| Equipment | Headphones, quiet room. Laptop speakers invalidate the quantization arm — the differences are small |
| Who | Normal-hearing adults. No audio expertise needed; ACR is designed for naive listeners |
| Network | Raters on the same LAN as the machine running the app, or a tunnel |

Recruiting 20 classmates for 15 minutes is realistic in a single afternoon. That is the whole
remaining cost of Phase D.

#### Why 18–20 and not 13

The dry run (15 simulated raters) put 91 paired observations on the table against a requirement of
~88 to detect a 0.3 MOS difference at 80 % power — i.e. **exactly at the threshold**. Two of the 15
were dropped by attention-check filtering, which is a normal rate.

So budget for attrition: **20 recruited → ~17 kept → comfortable margin.** At 13 or fewer the
`analyze_mos.py` output will label the study a pilot and the equivalence result will be
inconclusive rather than negative, which is a much weaker thing to report.

#### Running it

```bash
python mos_test/app.py
```

Serves on port **7861**. Raters open `http://<your-ip>:7861`, enter any rater id, and rate all 50
clips on the ACR 1–5 scale. Responses append to `mos_test/responses.csv`.

Verified working against the current 50-clip manifest: all clips serve, and **condition labels are
never sent to the browser** — the test is genuinely blind.

##### Instructions to give raters

> Rate the **quality of the speech** on each clip from 1 (bad) to 5 (excellent). Some clips are
> noisy, some have been processed. Judge how pleasant and clear the speech sounds overall. Use
> headphones. Do not go back and change earlier ratings.

Do not tell them what the conditions are, that some clips are near-identical, or which comparison
matters. That is the point of blinding.

#### What the test answers

50 clips = 7 utterances × 7 conditions + 1 attention check.

**Arm 1 — complex vs magnitude mask.** SI-SDR says the complex mask wins (+0.313 dB, p < 1e-6);
PESQ says it loses (−0.016, p = 0.005). The metrics contradict each other, so a human verdict
settles which one tracked perception.

**Arm 2 — FP32 vs mixed vs uniform INT8.** The paper claims the stem-exempt mixed model is
*indistinguishable* from FP32. That currently rests on p = 0.25, which is a **failure to reject**,
not a demonstration of sameness — an underpowered study produces the same p-value. Human ratings
plus an equivalence test convert this into a positive result.

The expected pattern, if the paper's claim holds:

| comparison | expected |
|---|---|
| `quant_fp32` vs `quant_mixed` | **equivalent** within ±0.3 MOS |
| `quant_fp32` vs `quant_uniform_int8` | uniform audibly **worse** |
| `quant_mixed` vs `quant_uniform_int8` | mixed **better** |

**If listeners do separate mixed from FP32, the claim must be softened.** Say so; do not re-run
until it comes out right.

#### Analysis

```bash
python scripts/analyze_mos.py
```

Produces per-condition MOS with bootstrap CIs, paired comparisons, equivalence tests, and a
MOS-vs-STOI correlation.

Two things were added specifically for this test:

1. **Paired within (rater, utterance).** Every rater hears every utterance under every condition,
   so pairing removes rater severity and utterance difficulty from the error term. Far more
   sensitive than comparing marginal means at these sample sizes.
2. **TOST equivalence testing** against a **declared ±0.3 MOS margin** (override with
   `MOS_EQUIV_MARGIN`). Equivalence is concluded when the 90 % CI of the difference lies entirely
   inside the margin. The margin is stated up front rather than chosen after seeing the data — a
   reader can disagree with 0.3, but cannot accuse it of being fitted.

The script drops raters who fail the attention check, and prints a power note that flags the study
as under-powered if the paired sample is too small to support an equivalence conclusion.

#### Validation already done

The full pipeline was dry-run on 15 simulated raters with a known ground truth, and recovered it:

| comparison | simulated truth | recovered |
|---|---|---|
| gtcrn vs complex_gtcrn | tie | no significant difference ✓ |
| fp32 vs mixed | equivalent | **EQUIVALENT**, TOST p = 0.0091 ✓ |
| fp32 vs uniform INT8 | uniform worse | fp32 better, p = 0.002 ✓ |
| noisy vs gtcrn | enhancement helps | gtcrn better, p = 1.8e-11 ✓ |

A deliberately careless simulated rater was caught by the attention check. So the analysis is known
to work *before* anyone spends time rating — if it had broken after collection, the data could not
have been regenerated.

#### Reporting

- Under 10 raters: report as a **pilot listening study**, indicative only. The script says so.
- Report the equivalence margin in the paper text, not just the p-value.
- Report how many raters were excluded and why.
- If Arm 1 comes out a tie, that is a real finding: it means the SI-SDR/PESQ disagreement is below
  the perceptual threshold, and *neither* metric should have been trusted to call a winner.
